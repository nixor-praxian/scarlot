# Why it could be interesting to evolve the discovery skills

*A standalone rationale, not a plan. Intended to inform a future decision about whether, when, and how to expand the interview analysis and synthesis skills beyond their current form.*

## Where we are

Two skills carry the discovery work today. One turns an interview transcript into a structured record. The other turns an internal conversation into a sync record. Across ten user interviews and a handful of internal syncs, they have been enough to generate a saturated priority stack (safety and filtering, centralised blacklist, cognitive overload), a running list of ten open assumptions, a ledger of how evidence has moved over time, and a small set of candidate new problems that are ripe for validation.

The skills work. The question is whether the next phase of work (moving from problem discovery toward concept and assumption validation) is well served by the current tooling, or whether a small, targeted evolution would pay for itself.

## Where the current skills show strain

Three signals have appeared in recent interviews.

First, the priority stack has saturated. The top three problems are at gold tier with multiple sources. Continuing to re-ask the same discovery questions produces diminishing returns. What the skills currently do not help with is shifting gears toward testing specific concepts, probing for willingness to pay directly, and forcing closure on the topics interviewees have systematically not mentioned.

Second, candidate new problems (platform identity mismatch, client reputation threats, commitment-signal screening) sit in limbo. They have one source each. The skills have no structured way to test whether they are real opportunities or solutions wearing an opportunity costume.

Third, the current records are deep but not scannable. Producing them is slow, reading them in bulk for pattern work is slower. As the cohort grows, the effort to keep up compounds.

## What a methodology upgrade could offer

Teresa Torres, in *Continuous Discovery Habits*, names a small set of primitives that map cleanly onto these strains. The most relevant for the current stage:

- **Interview snapshot**, a one-page artefact produced alongside each record. Not a replacement for the deep record. A scannable per-interview summary that makes cohort review tractable.
- **Assumption taxonomy** (desirability, viability, feasibility, usability, ethical). A small amount of labelling that lets the team decide which assumptions need which kind of test, rather than treating them as a flat list.
- **Story-based question pools anchored to live assumptions**. The current interview guide is organised by topic; a version organised by the specific assumption each question tries to move would sharpen every session.
- **A concept-reaction step at the end of the session** with a structured willingness-to-pay probe. Until now, interviews have produced indirect signals on pricing (paid subscriptions, existing tool spend). A direct test against concrete concepts would generate the first commitment-currency data.
- **A silence checklist** that forces closure on the topics that have gone unmentioned across multiple interviews (safety check-in habits, post-appointment notes, messaging fragmentation, relationship with local non-governmental organisations).

None of these requires rebuilding the skills. Each is a localised addition that could be tested in a single interview before being adopted more broadly.

## What it would cost

Three honest costs. First, time to write and review the updates to the skill reference files. Second, time to teach the new conventions to whoever is running the next interview (essentially: yourselves). Third, risk of over-engineering: each added primitive is another piece of process to maintain, and the team has explicit preferences against bloat.

The smaller the increment, the better the odds of sticking. A sensible sequence would pilot one primitive (probably the concept-reaction step, because it unblocks the clearest stuck decision) on the next interview, observe, then decide whether the others follow.

## Decisions a future evolution would need

Before any of this is worth doing, the team would need a view on:

1. Whether the next phase of user research is still broad discovery, or whether it is shifting toward concept validation. The answer determines which primitives are worth adding now versus later.
2. Whether the existing structured record stays as is, or whether adding a scannable layer above it is worth the extra writing pass.
3. How much methodology borrowing feels right. Torres is one source among several. Mom Test remains the operational spine. Any evolution should strengthen that spine rather than compete with it.

If and when these are settled, the scope of the evolution work can be drawn tightly around the answers. Until then, the skills as they stand continue to do the job they were built for.
